Problem statement
In Gulf family-led introductions, the questions that actually decide the outcome — daily religious practice, where the couple will live, whether the bride keeps her career, when children come, how money is arranged — are the ones nobody asks out loud. They surface after the meeting, after expectations have been set, and after refusing has become socially expensive for both households. The matchmaking office in the middle sees names and reputations, not the answers that matter, so it cannot tell an aligned pair from a mismatched one.
Solution overview
Kashf moves the hard questions in front of the introduction instead of after it. Each family answers twelve bilingual questions across two sealed stages — Foundational values, then Practical life — and neither side sees anything until both have finished. What is released is an alignment summary, never the raw answers: a readiness score against a published gate, four scored dimensions, and an explicit split between where the families agree and where they differ. Clear the gate and the app arranges the meeting itself.
The creative twist
Every escape route is designed as carefully as the happy path. A family can pause at any point and the other side is told only that the process is on hold — no reason is shared, and nothing is lost. Concerns go to a reviewer, not to the other family. When the gate fails, both households get the same detail-free message and the introduction closes cleanly. In a setting where refusing can cost a family its standing, making withdrawal cheap and face-preserving is the real product — it is what turns a scoring engine into something a family would agree to use at all.

How the filter actually works

Two mechanisms carry the product: a set of absolute blockers checked before anything begins, and a sealed reveal that releases only derived summaries.

Before scoring — the blockers

Declared at intake. “A match is stopped before it begins if it crosses any of these. Choose only what you would truly refuse.”
✕Living outside the Gulf
✕Living within the family compound
✕Bride leaving her career
✕Delaying children beyond three years
✕Second marriage in future
✕Smoking in the household

The sealed reveal

Nothing crosses between the families until both sides finish a stage — and even then, only a summary.
1Each family answers a stage privately — scales, choices, rankings and free text, every question in English and Arabic
2Both sides see progress only: “Stage submitted” against “Still answering”
3On mutual completion the summary opens for both at the same moment — four dimensions, never raw answers
4Free-text disclosures are routed to a human reviewer rather than scored
5Contact details are exchanged only once both families accept a meeting

The hard problem solved
Building a compatibility score that stays legible without ever exposing the answers behind it. Committing to mutual-completion gating means the product has to be useful while showing each family only a derived summary — so Kashf scores four named dimensions (Faith & values, Family expectations, Financial arrangements, Lifestyle & daily life), publishes the threshold it is judging against rather than hiding it, and splits the result into “Where you agree” and “Where you differ” so a weak dimension reads as an agenda item — “worth discussing plainly before anything is arranged” — instead of a verdict. Weak dimensions even render in gold rather than green, marking them without condemning them.
The same discipline extends to the operator. The matchmaking office console — بيت الوصال, read-only — lists cases by reference (KF-1179, KF-1166), showing stage, score and review flags, and states the constraint on its own face: “Stage and score only. Advisors never see the families' raw answers.” The business that runs Kashf is held to the same privacy rule as the families using it.
Worth noting for judging: the flow does not stop at a score. Clearing the gate opens meeting coordination built for the actual setting — dates in the Hijri calendar timed to prayer (Thursday 14 Rajab, evening, after Maghrib), a venue shortlist led by a private majlis with separate family seating, and a protocol both households confirm: chaperone agreed, ninety minutes, no photography, no third parties invited.
